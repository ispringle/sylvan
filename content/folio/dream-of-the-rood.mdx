# The Dream of the Rood

<div class="folio-lede">

<Illustration
  src={ruthwell}
  alt="The Ruthwell Cross, an early medieval free-standing stone cross"
  credit="The Ruthwell Cross (north face)"
  source="David Dixon / Geograph"
  float="right"
/>

*The Dream of the Rood* is an Old English dream-vision: a sleeper sees a
wondrous tree, wound in light and gold, that is also a cross of blood and
glory. The cross itself speaks. It tells how it was felled, raised, and forced
to become the instrument of a Lord's death, and how that death became victory.

The fullest text survives in the tenth-century **Vercelli Book**. Lines closely
related to the poem are also carved in runes on the eighth-century **Ruthwell
Cross** in Dumfriesshire. Poem and monument together make one of the richest
survivals of early English Christian imagination.

A distant modern echo is Angela Elwell Hunt's children's book *The Tale of
Three Trees* (1989): three trees dream of glory and are reshaped, against their
first hopes, into the manger, the fishing boat, and the cross. It is not a
retelling of *The Dream of the Rood*, but something of a spiritual successor —
the same instinct that a tree's life might be gathered into the story of
Christ, told so that a child can hear it.
